Concept map

Periodic trends arise from the competition between nuclear charge, shielding and electron distance.

Effective nuclear charge

It is approximated here with Slater's rules. A higher Zeff tends to attract valence electrons more strongly.

Radium

In general it decreases to the right across a period and increases going down a group, although there are nuances by block and by the definition of radius.

Ionization energy

In general it increases to the right and upward. There are exceptions associated with subshells and electron pairing.

Electronegativity

On the Pauling scale the general trend increases to the right and upward; noble gases are usually treated separately.

Electron affinity

Its trend is less regular than that of electronegativity or radius; it should not be learned as an absolute arrow.

Ionic radius

There is no single value per element: it depends on the charge, the oxidation state and the coordination. Here the Shannon effective radius of the most common ion in 6-coordination is shown as a comparative reference; take it as a representative value, not an absolute one.

Useful exceptions to discuss: Be/B and Mg/Al in first ionization; N/O because of pairing in 2p; electron affinity shows important irregularities; and in transition metals the trends are less simple.

Data sources: Pauling electronegativity (CRC Handbook / WebElements, via the Wikipedia data page); 1st ionization energy (NIST); electron affinity (compiled experimental values, Ning & Lu 2022); ionic radius (Shannon 1976, common ion, 6-coordination). Lead uses 1.87 (Pb²⁺); in tables that consider Pb⁴⁺ it appears as 2.33. Elements with “no data” have no established value or do not form a stable anion.
