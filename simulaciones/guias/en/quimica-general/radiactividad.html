Learning objectives

By the end, the student will be able to:

  • Balance nuclear equations.
  • Calculate decay with t½.
  • Apply ¹⁴C dating.

Prior knowledge

  • Atomic number and mass number.
  • Exponentials.

Suggested activity

Age of a sample

  1. With the simulation choose an isotope and its t½.
  2. Record N versus t.
  3. Determine the fraction remaining at 1, 2 and 3 t½.
  4. Calculate the age of a sample with 30 % of its ¹⁴C.

Common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrection
“After 2 t½ everything has decayed.”25 % remains.
“Half-life depends on the amount.”It is a constant of the isotope.
“γ radiation changes Z.”It changes neither Z nor A.

B. Essay

1. A sample has 12.5 % of the initial ¹⁴C. Calculate its age (t½ = 5730 years).

2. Complete: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ?

Answer key

Multiple choice

  1. C. Z−2 y A−4 — ⁴₂He is emitted.
  2. B. a neutron turns into a proton — n → p + e⁻ + ν̄; Z increases by 1.
  3. A. β⁺ (¹⁸O) — It has an excess of protons: p → n + e⁺ + ν.
  4. D. 1/8 — (1/2)³ = 1/8.
  5. C. λ = ln2 / t½ — From N = N₀e^(−λt) with N = N₀/2.
  6. B. ≈ 11,460 years — Two half-lives.
  7. A. ≈ 1,5 — More neutrons offset the electric repulsion.
  8. D. A and the charge — The total mass number and the total charge are conserved.
  9. A. ²³⁴Th — Z − 2, A − 4.
  10. C. 1/16 — (1/2)⁴.
  11. B. organic matter up to ≈ 50,000 years old — t½ ≈ 5730 years.

Essay (marking guide)

  1. 12.5 % = (1/2)³ → 3 × 5730 = 17,190 years.
  2. A β⁻ particle (electron) and an antineutrino.
