Isomerism · 3D Lab

Same formula, different structure: cis/trans and fac/mer isomers in 3D models, and Werner's argument for deducing geometry by counting isomers.

Open the simulation

⏱ 45 minLevel: undergraduate, early years3 multiple-choice questions · 2 essay questions

Learning objectives

By the end, the student will be able to:

  • Identify cis/trans and fac/mer isomers.
  • Count isomers in octahedral and square-planar geometries.
  • Apply Werner's argument.

Prior knowledge

  • Complexes and ligands.
  • Coordination geometries.

Suggested activity

Count isomers

  1. Draw MA₂B₄ and MA₃B₃ in an octahedron.
  2. Count the possible isomers.
  3. Contrast with a planar hexagon and a trigonal prism.
  4. Conclude how the geometry is deduced.

Common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrection
“cis and trans are enantiomers.”They are diastereomers.
“MA₂B₄ has three isomers.”It has two (cis and trans).
“Isomers share identical properties.”They differ in color, solubility and dipole moment.

B. Essay

1. Explain Werner's argument with [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺.

2. Cisplatin versus transplatin: why is only one antitumor?

Answer key

Multiple choice

  1. B. 2 — fac and mer.
  2. B. 2 — cis and trans.
  3. B. zero — Symmetry.

Essay (marking guide)

  1. Only 2 isomers are obtained: this matches the octahedron; a planar hexagon would give 3 and a trigonal prism 3.
  2. The cis binds to two adjacent DNA bases; the trans cannot.
